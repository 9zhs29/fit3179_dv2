Made in Australia

A record $2.7 billion was spent making films and TV dramas in Australia last year. Where did that money come from, where was it spent, and do Australians actually watch what we make?

By Ewan Chong (34996478)· FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 · October 2026

SCENE 1 — THE MONEY

Most of the money is now foreign

In 2024/25, drama production in Australia (feature films, TV and streaming series) hit $2.7 billion, the highest figure Screen Australia has ever recorded.

But for every $100 spent, about $60 came from international productions: overseas studios that shoot here or do their visual effects here. Australian stories made up the other $40.

SCENE 2 — BOOM, BUST, BOOM

A rollercoaster decade

Spending has swung wildly. Australia's early COVID control made it a "safe haven" for Hollywood in 2020/21. Then the 2023 US writers' and actors' strikes stopped many productions, and 2023/24 dropped to $1.7 billion. International shoots drove the record rebound in 2024/25.

Source: Screen Australia, Drama Reports 2022/23, 2023/24, 2024/25. Spend is counted in the financial year production started. 2024/25 international figure = total minus Australian titles.

SCENE 3 — THE STATE RACE

Queensland steals the crown

For years, New South Wales was the undisputed home of Australian screen production. In 2024/25, Queensland overtook it for the first time in this period, thanks to blockbusters filmed at the Gold Coast's studios.

A one-year swing

NSW's share of national spending fell from about half to under a third in a single year. Queensland's doubled. Big international shoots move from state to state, so one or two films can reshape the whole map.

ACT, NT and Tasmania are reported together by Screen Australia to protect confidentiality.

SCENE 4 — ON THE MAP

Per person, Queensland is the screen state

Big states will always spend more in total. Dividing each state's spend by its population shows how much the industry matters locally. Queensland's $925 million works out to about $161 for every resident, well ahead of Victoria and NSW.

Sources: Screen Australia Drama Reports; Australian Bureau of Statistics, National, state and territory population (March 2026).

SCENE 5 — WHO PAYS?

Taxpayers fund a third or more of our stories

Australian films and series rarely pay for themselves upfront. Government money covers 44% of local feature film budgets, mostly through the Producer Offset, a federal tax rebate that returns part of a production's budget to the producer. TV and streaming series lean more on broadcasters and streamers.

Source: Screen Australia, Drama Report 2024/25 Key Findings.

SCENE 6 — GOING GLOBAL

Our co-production partners

An official co-production is a film or series made under a treaty between Australia and another country, so it counts as "local" in both. Since 1986, 230 titles have been made this way. Canada, the UK and France account for more than 70% of them.

Source: Screen Australia, Co-production trends (as at 30 June 2026).

SCENE 7 — THE AUDIENCE

We make them here, but we rarely watch them

Here's the twist. Australia is busier than ever as a place to make films, but Australian films usually earn only 2–5% of what we spend at the cinema. Big years come from one or two breakout hits: Crocodile Dundee in 1986, or Peter Rabbit 2 and The Dry in 2021.

Hollywood takes the lion's share

Over the last three years, US films took about 84 cents of every box-office dollar. Australian films took under 3 cents, their lowest share in this period. Many of the record-breaking productions in Scene 1 were Hollywood films that happened to be shot here.

Three-year averages smooth out single breakout years.

Source: Screen Australia, Domestic box office and share since 1977; Top 100 Australian feature films of all time (box office in current dollars, not adjusted for inflation).

Australia has never spent more making screen drama, but about 60% of that money now comes from overseas productions, and Queensland has overtaken New South Wales as the biggest production state. Australian-made films and series still rely heavily on government support, which covers 44% of local feature film budgets. Yet at the cinema, Australian films took just 2.8% of ticket sales over 2023–25, while US films took about 84%. In short, Australia is a busy place to make films, but much of what is made here is made by and for overseas studios.
